# Discrimination, harassment and victimisation (Equality Act 2010)

## 1. Who is protected

Employees, workers, contract workers, job applicants, partners, office holders and many self-employed people who contract personally to do work (EqA s.83 — wide definition of "employment"). **No qualifying period.** Former employees are protected against post-employment discrimination (s.108).

## 2. Nine protected characteristics (ss.4–12)

Age; disability; gender reassignment; marriage and civil partnership (direct discrimination and harassment limits apply); pregnancy and maternity; race (colour, nationality, ethnic or national origins); religion or belief (including philosophical belief meeting the Grainger criteria — Grainger v Nicholson [2010]; Forstater v CGD Europe [2021]); sex; sexual orientation.

"Sex" in the Equality Act means **biological sex** (For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers [2025] UKSC 16). Trans people remain protected on the ground of gender reassignment, including by perception and association.

**Disability** (s.6, Sch. 1): a physical or mental impairment with a substantial (more than minor or trivial) and long-term (12 months or likely to last 12 months, or recurring) adverse effect on normal day-to-day activities. Cancer, HIV and multiple sclerosis are disabilities from diagnosis. Effects of treatment are disregarded.

## 3. Prohibited conduct

| Type | Definition | Justification defence? |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct discrimination** (s.13) | Less favourable treatment because of a characteristic (including perception and association) | Only for age (proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim) |
| **Indirect discrimination** (s.19) | A provision, criterion or practice (PCP) applied to all that puts people sharing a characteristic at a particular disadvantage and puts the claimant at that disadvantage | Yes — proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim |
| **Discrimination arising from disability** (s.15) | Unfavourable treatment because of something arising in consequence of disability (e.g. sickness absence), where the employer knew or could reasonably be expected to know of the disability | Yes — proportionality |
| **Failure to make reasonable adjustments** (ss.20–21, Sch. 8) | Duty to take reasonable steps to avoid substantial disadvantage from a PCP, physical feature or lack of an auxiliary aid | No (only reasonableness of the step) |
| **Harassment** (s.26) | Unwanted conduct related to a characteristic, or of a sexual nature, with the purpose or effect of violating dignity or creating an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment (effect judged on the claimant's perception, other circumstances and reasonableness) | No |
| **Victimisation** (s.27) | Detriment because the person did a protected act (brought proceedings, gave evidence, made an allegation of breach of the Act) or is believed to have done so — unless a false allegation made in bad faith | No |
| **Pregnancy and maternity discrimination** (s.18) | Unfavourable treatment because of pregnancy or illness resulting from it during the protected period, or because of maternity leave | No — no comparator needed |

## 4. Burden of proof (s.136)

If the claimant proves facts from which the tribunal could decide, absent another explanation, that discrimination occurred, the burden shifts to the employer to prove it did not (Igen v Wong [2005] EWCA Civ 142; Efobi v Royal Mail [2021] UKSC 33). Something more than a difference in status and a difference in treatment is usually needed (Madarassy v Nomura [2007] EWCA Civ 33).

## 5. Sexual harassment — strengthened duties

- Employers must take **all reasonable steps** to prevent sexual harassment of their employees in the course of employment (s.40A; "all" added from **30 October 2026** by ERA 2025 s.20). Breach is enforced by the EHRC and by a **compensation uplift of up to 25%** in a successful sexual harassment claim (s.124A).
- From **30 October 2026** employers are liable for **harassment by third parties** (customers, clients, contractors) related to any relevant characteristic if they failed to take all reasonable steps to prevent it (s.40(1A)–(1C)).
- Regulations may specify steps that are to be regarded as reasonable (s.40B).
- Since 6 April 2026 a disclosure about sexual harassment can be a **protected disclosure** for whistleblowing purposes (ERA 2025 s.23).
- Confidentiality clauses (NDAs) preventing disclosure of harassment or discrimination will be void (ERA 2025 s.24) — expected **September 2027**.

Practical steps employers are expected to take: risk assessment, clear policy, training, reporting channels, prompt investigation, protecting complainants, monitoring (EHRC technical guidance).

## 6. Equal pay (ss.64–80)

A **sex equality clause** is implied into every contract: equal pay for **like work**, **work rated as equivalent** or **work of equal value** with a comparator of the opposite sex in the same employment (same establishment or common terms). Material factor defence (s.69). Claims up to 6 months after the employment ends (s.129); arrears up to 6 years (5 in Scotland). Pay secrecy clauses are unenforceable against "relevant pay disclosures" (s.77). Gender pay gap reporting for employers with 250+ employees; equality action plans (gender pay gap and menopause) under ERA 2025 s.33 — voluntary from April 2026, expected to become mandatory from 2027.

## 7. Remedies (s.124)

- Declaration, recommendation, **compensation without a cap**: financial loss (past and future, with mitigation) plus **injury to feelings** — Vento bands (Presidential Guidance, claims presented on or after 6 April 2026): lower £1,300–£12,600; middle £12,600–£37,700; upper £37,700–£62,900; above £62,900 in exceptional cases. Aggravated damages, personal injury (psychiatric harm), interest.
- Individual respondents (managers, colleagues) can be personally liable (ss.110, 111, 112).
- Employer's defence for acts of employees: it took all reasonable steps to prevent the employee doing that thing or anything of that description (s.109(4)) — training must be effective and refreshed (Allay v Gehlen [2021]).

## 8. Time limit and procedure

6 months from the act (relevant dates from 1 October 2026; 3 months before), conduct extending over a period treated as done at the end; extension where **just and equitable** (s.123). Acas early conciliation first. Pre-claim questions: the statutory questionnaire procedure was abolished in 2014, but the claimant can still ask written questions and the tribunal may draw inferences from evasive answers.

## 9. Reasonable adjustments — typical examples

Phased return, altered hours or duties, home working, equipment or software, adjusting absence trigger points, allowing time off for treatment, redeployment to a suitable vacancy without competitive interview (Archibald v Fife Council [2004] UKHL 32), modified procedures for hearings. Access to Work can fund equipment and support.
