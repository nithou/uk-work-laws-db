# Sickness absence and statutory sick pay

## 1. Statutory sick pay (SSP) — rules from 6 April 2026

| Rule | Position from 6 April 2026 (ERA 2025 ss.10–13; SI 2026/373) |
|---|---|
| Who | Employees (employed earners), including agency workers and many zero-hours workers |
| Earnings condition | **Removed** — no lower earnings limit |
| Waiting days | **Removed** — SSP is payable from the **first** qualifying day of incapacity |
| Rate | The **lower of £123.25 a week or 80% of normal weekly earnings** (SSCBA s.157) |
| Duration | Up to 28 weeks in a period of incapacity (linked periods: 2+ days separated by 8 weeks or less) |
| Evidence | Self-certification for the first 7 days; fit note (from a doctor, nurse, pharmacist, occupational therapist or physiotherapist) after that |
| Enforcement | Employer disputes: HMRC decision on entitlement (SSP is not normally a tribunal claim, though non-payment of contractual sick pay is a deductions/contract claim). Fair Work Agency to take on SSP enforcement (date not confirmed) |

Contractual (occupational) sick pay is often more generous: check the contract and handbook, and any discretion clauses (discretion must be exercised rationally).

## 2. Managing absence fairly

- **Short-term, intermittent absence**: fair warning, review meetings, consider underlying cause (disability?), give an opportunity to improve; trigger-point policies must be applied with adjustments for disability where reasonable.
- **Long-term absence**: consult the employee, obtain up-to-date medical evidence (occupational health; GP report needs consent under the Access to Medical Reports Act 1988), consider adjustments and alternative roles, and decide whether the employer can reasonably wait longer (Daubney; Spencer v Paragon Wallpapers [1977]; BS v Dundee City Council [2013] CSIH 91 — relevant factors include length of absence, prospects of return, effect on the business and whether sick pay is exhausted).
- Dismissal for ill-health capability can be fair under ERA s.98(2)(a), but must be **proportionate** if the employee is disabled (discrimination arising from disability, s.15 EqA — O'Brien v Bolton St Catherine's Academy [2017] EWCA Civ 145 aligns the tests).
- An employee on long-term sick leave keeps accruing statutory holiday and may carry it over (WTR reg. 13(10)–(11)).
- **Permanent health insurance / income protection**: dismissing an employee to deprive them of benefits under a PHI scheme can be a breach of an implied term (Aspden v Webbs Poultry [1996]).

## 3. Fit notes and return to work

The fit note may say "not fit for work" or "may be fit for work" with suggested adjustments (phased return, altered hours, amended duties, workplace adaptations). If the employer cannot accommodate the advice, the employee is treated as not fit. Occupational health referral and a return-to-work meeting are good practice.

## 4. Disability: reasonable adjustments in absence cases

Examples: discounting disability-related absence from triggers, longer phased return, home working, change of duties or hours, redeployment, paid time off for treatment, delaying formal stages. Failure to adjust is unlawful regardless of justification; unfavourable treatment because of absence arising from disability must be justified.

## 5. Time off for dependants and emergencies

Reasonable unpaid time off to deal with an emergency involving a dependant (illness, injury, childbirth, breakdown of care arrangements, death, incidents at school) — ERA s.57A, day one, protection against detriment and dismissal.
