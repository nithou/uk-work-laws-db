# The law on industrial action – Strikes and industrial action

_Source: Acas — https://www.acas.org.uk/strikes-and-industrial-action_

Industrial action is when workers take action against their employer because of a work dispute. This is usually organised by a trade union.

Types of industrial action include:

- striking
- picketing
- action short of a strike, for example refusing to work overtime

In some situations, an employer might take industrial action against workers. For example, a 'lockout' to stop workers from working.

Industrial action is usually a last resort when other ways of negotiating have not worked.

Workers, employers and trade unions must follow industrial action law, mainly covered by the:

- Trade Union and Labour Relations (Consolidation) Act 1992
- Employment Rights Act 1996
- Employment Rights Act 2025

## Official action

Official industrial action is when a trade union has followed all the legal rules. This includes holding a ballot for members to vote on taking industrial action.

Workers are protected by law when taking official industrial action.

### Striking

This is when workers take action by refusing to work.

[Find out more about striking](https://www.acas.org.uk/strikes-and-industrial-action/strikes "Strikes")

### Picketing

This is when workers and trade union representatives stand outside a workplace to tell people why they are striking.

[Find out more about picketing](https://www.acas.org.uk/strikes-and-industrial-action/picketing "Picketing")

### Action short of a strike

This is when workers continue working but withdraw some of their labour.

For example:

- 'working to rule' – this means refusing to do work that is optional in their contract, for example refusing to work overtime
- 'go slow' or 'slowdown' – this means deliberately slowing down their work
- refusing to do a specific task or part of the job

If the trade union has followed a legal ballot process, workers are protected by industrial action law.

Taking action short of a strike does not usually break the work contract.

If you need more advice about contracts and pay during action short of strike, [contact the Acas helpline](https://www.acas.org.uk/contact "Contact us").

## Unofficial action

Unofficial industrial action is when either:

- a trade union takes action without following the legal rules – for example instructing members to strike without holding a ballot
- workers take action that's not authorised by a trade union

If workers take unofficial action, they are not protected by industrial action law. However, they'll still have their usual employment rights.

Examples of unofficial action include:

- secondary action
- unofficial or 'wildcat' strikes

### Secondary action

Secondary action is when workers take action in support of striking workers at a different workplace. This is sometimes called 'sympathy action'.

For example, workers might refuse to:

- cross a picket line and enter the workplace
- handle goods or services from the employer involved in the dispute

### Unofficial strikes

A strike is unofficial when either:

- a trade union strikes without following the legal rules – for example not holding a ballot
- workers strike without involving their trade union – sometimes called a 'wildcat strike'

### If someone takes unofficial action

Workers who take unofficial action are not protected by industrial action law. This means they could be fairly dismissed for taking unofficial action.

However, they'll still have their usual employment rights.

Employers must follow a fair disciplinary process before taking action against a worker involved in unofficial action.

For example, a delivery driver refuses to cross the picket line of a different employer. The driver's employer is responsible for deciding on any disciplinary action. They must follow a full and fair procedure.

If you're considering taking unofficial action, you can:

- talk to your trade union representative for advice
- [contact the Acas helpline](https://www.acas.org.uk/contact "Contact us") to talk through your options

## Holding a ballot

To take official action, trade unions must hold a ballot for their members.

The ballot asks members to vote on taking industrial action.

To follow the legal rules, the trade union must:

- hold the ballot before taking industrial action
- give the employer one week's notice of the ballot starting
- invite all eligible members to vote
- use a postal, electronic or in-person ballot
- share the results with everyone who was eligible to vote
- tell the employer the result as soon as possible
- have an independent ballot supervisor, if more than 50 members are voting

For the ballot to succeed:

- at least 50% of eligible members must respond
- more members must vote in support of industrial action than against it

A trade union does not have to hold another ballot if either:

- the type of official action changes
- the action lasts longer than expected

### Taking action after a ballot

If a ballot is successful, the trade union has 12 months to take action. If they want to take action after that, they'll need to hold another ballot.

If action takes place without a legal ballot:

- the trade union could face legal action or be fined
- workers would not be protected from unfair dismissal if they took action

For more information about balloting rules, check:

- [the Code of Practice for industrial action ballots on GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/code-of-practice-industrial-action-ballots-and-notice-to-employers)
- [holding a ballot on GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/industrial-action-strikes/holding-a-ballot), including how to complain about a ballot

## Who can take part in industrial action

If the ballot is successful, workers in the 'bargaining unit' can take part in industrial action.

The bargaining unit is the group of workers the trade union negotiates for through a 'collective agreement'. This includes:

- trade union members who were asked to vote in the ballot – it does not matter if they voted, or how they voted
- non-union members who cannot vote, but can join any official action

To check if a collective agreement is in place, either:

- check the worker's contract
- ask the trade union or employer

This could cover all workers in an organisation, or a smaller group.

Trade union representatives can also take part in industrial action.

## Dismissal and less favourable treatment

Workers taking part in an official strike and other industrial action have extra legal rights.

### Less favourable treatment

Employers must not cause 'detriment' to anyone who takes part in official industrial action.

Detriment means someone experiences one or both of the following:

- being treated worse than before
- having their situation made worse

Examples of detriment could be:

- they experience [bullying](https://www.acas.org.uk/bullying-at-work "Bullying at work")
- they experience [harassment](https://www.acas.org.uk/discrimination-and-the-law/harassment "Harassment")
- their employer turns down their training requests without good reason
- they are overlooked for promotions or development opportunities
- their employer reduces their hours without good reason

### Unfair dismissal

Anyone with the [legal status of employee](https://www.acas.org.uk/employment-status/employee-employment-status "Employee") is protected from unfair dismissal if they take part in official industrial action.

Someone is not likely to be an employee if they're:

- an agency worker
- a casual worker
- on a zero-hours contract

If someone has the [legal status of worker](https://www.acas.org.uk/employment-status/worker-employment-status "Worker"), they cannot claim unfair dismissal. However, they might be able to claim that dismissal for taking part in industrial action was a detriment.

[Find out more about unfair dismissal](https://www.acas.org.uk/dismissals/unfair-dismissal "Unfair dismissal")

## Get advice and support

As a worker, you can talk to your trade union representative if you're a member.

You can contact an employers' association for advice, if you're a member. For example:

- the Chartered Management Institute (CMI)
- the Federation of Small Businesses (FSB)

Employers and workers can [contact the Acas helpline](https://www.acas.org.uk/contact "Contact us"). We can talk through:

- any questions about strikes and industrial action
- your dispute and how we might help find a solution
